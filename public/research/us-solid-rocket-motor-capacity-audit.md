# America’s Solid-Rocket-Motor Buildout Is Real. The Capacity Numbers Are Not Additive.

A plant-by-plant audit of 2018-2029 expansion claims shows more tactical suppliers and more propellant capability, but no defensible national total and no broad escape from program qualification or upstream concentration.

Research cutoff: 2026.08.16. Publication edition: 2026.09.06.

Public-source audit through August 16, 2026. Company releases establish what operators disclosed, not independently verified achieved rates. The research does not calculate wartime sufficiency, classified demand, inventory, product-mix-adjusted national output, yield, or accepted deliveries by facility. Readiness scores are derived public-evidence judgments, not engineering certifications.

The United States is building substantially more solid-rocket-motor capacity. Northrop Grumman says it can process 30 million pounds of propellant a year today and is moving toward nearly 50 million pounds by 2028. L3Harris is constructing a large-motor complex in Camden, Arkansas, while planning a more than $1 billion expansion in Orange County, Virginia. Anduril has opened a tactical-motor plant in Mississippi. X-Bow has installed modular energetics equipment in Texas. Ursa Major has completed a Navy-funded manufacturing pathfinder in Colorado. Prometheus Energetics broke ground on a four-line campus in Indiana. American Pacific is expanding the concentrated domestic ammonium-perchlorate base by more than 50 percent. [S05-S09, S12-S24]

Those facts do not produce a national “motors per year” total. The 31 facility and capability records collected for this article use 11 different metric families: motor counts, pounds of energetics, tons with no stated time basis, production multipliers, percentage changes, facility area, capital commitments, test milestones, qualification milestones, scenario conversions, and claims with no normalized output. The motor counts themselves cover products ranging from palm-sized devices to truck-sized stages. Adding them would create precision without meaning.

The strongest supportable conclusion is narrower and more useful. By 2029, the United States should have materially more physical manufacturing capability and a broader set of tactical and some medium-class suppliers. Incumbents are likely to provide most of the usable near-term increase because they already possess qualified designs, trained workforces, sub-tier relationships, and active production programs. New entrants are making real progress, but public evidence does not show interchangeable, program-qualified competition across large and strategic motor classes. The buildout attacks one layer of the problem. Program qualification, test capacity, ammonium perchlorate, HTPB binder, and third-tier components remain separate gates.

That distinction matters for procurement. A plant can be operational but not qualified for the motor a program needs. A supplier can cast propellant but lack a qualified nozzle, case, igniter, liner, or acceptance process. A company can report a production multiplier without disclosing the starting rate. A government can fund a second source, yet receive no deployable motor until validation, qualification, and system integration are complete. “More capacity” is directionally true. “The bottleneck is solved” is not supported by the public record.

## Why the public numbers do not add

The capacity discussion often starts with two apparently clean figures. L3Harris says its Camden operations produce more than 115,000 solid rocket motors annually. Northrop says it expects to produce more than 25,000 motors annually in 2029. Read literally, L3 appears to produce more than four times Northrop’s future output. That interpretation is wrong.

L3 explicitly says the Camden count spans motors “from the size of a person’s palm to the size of a semi-truck.” Northrop does not publish the product mix behind its 25,000 figure. A small gas generator, tactical booster, interceptor motor, and large strategic stage do not consume similar energetic mass, labor, test capacity, floor space, or time. Motor count is a valid operating statistic inside a defined portfolio. It is not a cross-company capacity denominator without product mix. [S05-S06]

Propellant mass is cleaner, but only up to a point. Northrop’s company-wide disclosure moves from 30 million pounds a year to nearly 50 million by 2028, an approximate 66.7 percent increase. That is a meaningful measure of energetic throughput. It still does not reveal how much becomes tactical motors, large stages, test articles, scrap, or work in process. Nor does it establish final motor deliveries, because cases, nozzles, igniters, liners, insulation, inspection, aging, and acceptance remain outside the mass number. [S05]

X-Bow’s own conversion illustrates the problem better than any critique. The company says a future 3-million-pound annual energetics capability could support roughly 3,000 to 3,800 Standard Missile-class motors or 30,000 to 50,000 tactical motors. Those ranges imply approximately 789 to 1,000 pounds of energetics per assumed Standard Missile equivalent and 60 to 100 pounds per assumed tactical equivalent. The same mass supports an order-of-magnitude difference in motor count because the product is different. The conversion is useful as a scenario. It is not a bill of materials, a committed production mix, or a national capacity unit. [S18]

The other public measures are even less convertible. Northrop reports that it has tripled tactical capacity at Allegany Ballistics Laboratory, doubled large-motor production in Utah, tripled casting capability, increased one plant’s mix capacity 15 percent, and plans another 30 percent energetics increase. L3 says its Arkansas project will increase large-motor capacity sixfold, its Virginia project will more than double manufacturing space, and program frameworks could quadruple THAAD propulsion and nearly triple PAC-3 propulsion products. A multiplier without a denominator is not an output figure. Facility area is not throughput. A “propulsion product” can combine solid boost motors, attitude-control devices, liquid divert systems, and other components. [S05-S11]

Capital is not capacity either. Northrop says it has invested more than $1 billion since 2018. L3’s Camden project exceeds $400 million, its Virginia plan exceeds $1 billion, and a 2023 DoD agreement provided $215.6 million across Camden, Huntsville, and Orange County. Anduril disclosed $75 million of private investment plus Defense Production Act awards. Prometheus says up to $175 million is committed. AMPAC received approval for up to $100 million. These figures show commitment and physical intensity. They cannot be summed into an industry investment total without project-level cash-flow records because government awards, company totals, and later facility announcements can describe overlapping work. [S05-S09, S12-S17, S23-S25]

## The starting point was a two-producer structure

The current buildout is a response to a long consolidation cycle. GAO reported in 2017 that the number of U.S. solid-rocket-motor manufacturers fell from six in 1995 to two: Aerojet Rocketdyne and Orbital ATK. One industry estimate cited by GAO put the broader supplier base at roughly 5,000 companies in 1995 and about 1,000 by 2017. At the time, DoD believed forecast demand could sustain only two motor manufacturers. The industrial logic was understandable. Qualification is expensive, demand is cyclical, facilities are hazardous and specialized, and low rates raise unit cost. The result was a structure with little spare competitive depth. [S01]

Northrop’s 2018 acquisition of Orbital ATK intensified the vertical-integration concern. The Federal Trade Commission’s final order required Northrop to supply solid rocket motors and related services on a non-discriminatory basis and established a firewall around competitively sensitive information. In April 2026, the FTC sought comment on Northrop’s petition to set the order aside. The public case page did not show a later disposition by the August 16 cutoff. Lockheed Martin opposed removal, arguing that new entrants were not yet capable of supplying large motors at scale and that Northrop remained the only source for certain programs. Other participants argued the tactical market had become more competitive. [S02-S04, S30]

The apparent conflict is mainly a classification problem. The claim that the United States has a credible third tactical supplier can coexist with the claim that large, program-qualified sources remain concentrated. A market is not either competitive or concentrated in the abstract. It is competitive for a defined motor, configuration, performance envelope, qualification status, production rate, and delivery window.

## The incumbents are adding the most immediately usable capacity

## Northrop Grumman: the broadest disclosed energetic expansion

Northrop’s public expansion page is the most complete incumbent disclosure. The company says it has delivered more than 1.3 million motors, invested more than $1 billion in solid propulsion since 2018, and can produce more than 30 million pounds of propellant annually. It describes completed or planned work across Allegany Ballistics Laboratory, Utah, Elkton, and other locations. The company targets nearly 50 million pounds of annual propellant capability by 2028 and more than 25,000 motors annually in 2029, 25 percent above its stated 2028 level. The latter implies an approximate 20,000-motor comparison point for 2028, but both the “more than” and the undisclosed mix make the inference approximate. [S05]

Several facility disclosures are relevant but should not be added to the company-wide targets. Northrop says it tripled tactical capacity at Allegany, doubled large-motor production in Utah, tripled propellant casting, installed a 15 percent mix-capacity increment at ABL Plant III, and plans a 30 percent energetics increase at Plant V by 2027. The Plant V description includes 22 buildings and three 300-gallon mixers. Elkton is receiving a $100 million investment associated with a fivefold delivery increase for high-speed and hypersonic propulsion. These figures identify where the company is adding capability. They almost certainly overlap the 2028 and 2029 company totals, so treating them as separate increments would double count. [S05]

Northrop’s advantage is not just equipment. It has a portfolio of qualified programs, operating recipes, safety approvals, quality systems, suppliers, acceptance history, and customers that can absorb incremental output without a new-source qualification campaign for every existing motor. That makes incumbent expansion more likely to translate into accepted deliveries quickly. The trade-off is concentration: more output at Northrop improves throughput but does not give a prime contractor an independent alternative for a Northrop-only design.

## L3Harris/Aerojet Rocketdyne: a multi-site expansion tied to program demand

L3Harris is expanding across Arkansas, Virginia, and Alabama. At Camden, the company says it currently produces more than 115,000 motors each year across a mixed portfolio. Its Arkansas Advanced Propulsion Facilities project covers more than 20 buildings and 230,000 square feet, costs more than $400 million, and is intended to increase large-motor capacity sixfold. DoD’s 2023 $215.6 million agreement supported modernization at Camden, Huntsville, and Orange County for programs including Javelin, Stinger, and GMLRS. [S06-S07]

The Orange County, Virginia, expansion is larger in capital terms. L3 announced more than $1 billion for new Virginia Advanced Propulsion Facilities, more than doubling manufacturing space from a disclosed 256,000-square-foot base. The site is described as a small-to-medium motor center with mixing, grinding, casting, and final assembly. The company did not publish an annual motor target, so the proper record is a footprint and process-capability expansion, not an invented output estimate. [S08]

Huntsville adds inert-component capacity. L3 opened or expanded 130,000 square feet for approximately $25 million, bringing the local footprint to around 670,000 square feet. The site supports components used in more than half of the company’s motor programs. This matters because inert components can bind output even when mixing and casting are available. It is not, however, a direct energetic-throughput number. [S09]

Two July 2026 announcements provide unusually strong demand signals but not finalized quantities. L3 signed frameworks expected to lead to seven-year contracts that would quadruple THAAD propulsion production and nearly triple PAC-3 MSE propulsion-product output. The THAAD scope includes solid boost motors and liquid divert-and-attitude-control systems. The PAC-3 scope includes a two-pulse motor, attitude-control motors, and a lethality enhancer. Both releases said definitization was expected later in 2026. At the cutoff, they were frameworks, not finalized production orders, and their multipliers should not be treated as disclosed motor counts. [S10-S11]

The incumbents’ expansion is therefore substantial and relatively mature. It is also difficult to summarize in one figure. Northrop publishes propellant mass and mixed motor output. L3 publishes a mixed motor count, large-motor multipliers, floor area, and program-specific propulsion multipliers. Each can be true without being directly comparable.

## The entrants are real, but their evidence is class-specific

## Anduril: the strongest new tactical operating case

Among the new merchant suppliers, Anduril has the strongest public operating evidence for tactical motors. The company acquired Adranos and expanded the Mississippi Solid Rocket Motor Complex at McHenry. A January 2025 Defense Production Act award supplied $14.3 million alongside $75 million of private investment for mixing, casting, curing, quality assurance, and final assembly. Anduril said the expansion would move annual tactical capacity from 600 to more than 6,000 motors. [S12-S13]

In August 2025, the company described the plant as operational at full-rate-production capability, with more than 700 motors test-fired since January 2024, more than 100 employees, nine buildings, and 450 acres. It set an end-2026 target above 6,000 tactical motors per year. A product page gives a separate Denali line figure above 1,200 units per year. Unless the company explicitly states otherwise, the Denali line should be treated as part of the site’s broader capacity rather than an additional 1,200. [S14-S15]

The program evidence is advancing but incomplete. Saab selected Anduril to design, develop, qualify, and eventually produce a replacement motor for Ground-Launched Small Diameter Bomb, with a planned full-rate transition in 2026. Anduril also disclosed another $43.7 million DPA award for testing, storage, and tooling and said it had received an allied full-rate tactical air-defense production contract. Public quantities and independent evidence of the GLSDB qualification transition were not available at the cutoff. [S16-S17]

The right classification is production evidence in tactical motors, with named-program qualification still varying by product. It would be too skeptical to call McHenry merely a prototype shop. It would be too aggressive to infer that the plant can substitute for qualified large interceptor or strategic motors.

## X-Bow: meaningful mass capability, with the mix unresolved

X-Bow’s Luling, Texas, facility is the clearest new entrant disclosure expressed in energetic mass. In November 2025, the company said equipment installation and commissioning were under way for an initial one-million-pound annual capability, scaling to three million pounds within 12 to 24 months. The release cited roughly $40 million of private capital plus government co-investment. X-Bow also referenced development work connected to Mk72 and Mk104 motors. [S18]

The mass figure is valuable because it avoids mixing tiny and large motors in one count. It does not solve the qualification problem. The site can be physically capable of mixing and processing energetics while individual products remain in development, qualification, or customer acceptance. X-Bow’s missile-equivalent ranges are best read as sensitivity analysis supplied by the company itself. At three million pounds, a tactical-heavy mix creates tens of thousands of assumed motor equivalents; a Standard Missile-class mix creates only a few thousand. The national output question therefore cannot be answered until product mix and qualified demand are known.

X-Bow’s 2026 acquisition of Evolution Space could add assets and additional production claims. It is excluded from the core capacity ledger because the public disclosure did not permit a clean separation of operating status, motor mix, and overlap with X-Bow’s other expansion. Exclusion does not mean the asset is unimportant. It means the evidence is not comparable enough to put into a total. [S19]

## Ursa Major: flexible manufacturing and credible prototypes, not yet broad substitution

Ursa Major describes a flexible manufacturing system covering motors from 2 to 22 inches in diameter. Its public record includes static fires at 2.75, 5, 7, 10, and 13.5 inches, 11 live-fire flights, and production in the hundreds of motors per year. That is meaningful test and manufacturing evidence across a wider diameter envelope than a single tactical product. The public material does not disclose an at-rate delivery mix by program. [S20]

The company and the Navy made a $25 million joint investment in a 10-inch prototype pathfinder, with $12.5 million from the government matched by Ursa. In July 2026, Ursa said the pathfinder had completed on schedule in February, with the high-loading-grain prototype designed, manufactured, and static-fired to stated objectives. The company described the result as a foundation for future systems. That wording matters. A successful manufacturing pathfinder proves more than a slide deck and less than a qualified second source for a deployed missile. [S21-S22]

Ursa therefore sits at public-evidence level 2 in this article’s taxonomy: prototype and qualification evidence, with some production evidence at smaller scale, but no public basis to classify it alongside sustained program-qualified incumbents across its full advertised diameter range.

## Prometheus Energetics: the largest clean-sheet plan is still pre-production

Prometheus Energetics, a joint venture associated with Kratos and RAFAEL, broke ground in February 2026 on an approximately 600-acre campus in Bloomfield, Indiana. The company described four production lines, up to 800 tons of energetics capacity, and up to $175 million of committed capital. Production is projected to begin in 2027 after construction and certification. [S23]

This is a substantial physical commitment. It is also the clearest example of why source language must be preserved. The release does not define whether “800 tons” is annual capacity, batch inventory, total installed capacity, or another basis. Converting it to pounds per year or motors per year would silently add a time denominator that the source does not provide. The facility belongs in the audit as a planned clean-sheet capability at readiness level 1, not as annual national output.

## Firehawk: relevant watchlist, insufficient output evidence

Firehawk disclosed an agreement to acquire a 636-acre, DCMA-rated facility in Crawford, Mississippi, and described a 340-acre production campus under development in Lawton, Oklahoma. The company has used an aspirational thousands-per-month production frame. The public release does not supply a product-mix-adjusted annual output or evidence of completed at-rate production. The sites are included as a watchlist, not a normalized capacity contribution. [S33]

## Installed manufacturing is only the first gate

A motor plant must complete several different transitions before it relieves a procurement bottleneck.

First is physical capability: buildings, mixers, casting pits, cure ovens, machining, inspection, storage, test stands, safety systems, labor, and environmental approvals. Construction and equipment commissioning establish that the work can occur at a site.

Second is process capability: repeatable formulation, mixing, casting, cure, bond quality, dimensional control, non-destructive inspection, lot acceptance, and yield. Hundreds of successful test fires can be strong evidence here, but test consumption is not delivery.

Third is configuration qualification. The motor must be qualified for a specific design and source. Changes in propellant, case, nozzle, liner, igniter, manufacturing process, sub-tier component, or facility can trigger new evidence requirements. The customer must know that the source produces the required thrust-time curve, structural margin, aging behavior, environmental tolerance, insensitive-munitions performance, storage life, and system interactions.

Fourth is production acceptance and sustained rate: repeatable lots, customer acceptance, on-time deliveries, fielded reliability, and enough input material to maintain output. This is where labor, quality escapes, supplier interruptions, and test bottlenecks become visible.

Congress’s treatment of SM-6 motors shows that qualification is its own industrial-base program. The House Armed Services Committee reported that the Navy had awarded at least six contracts to validate and mature alternative Mk72 and Mk104 manufacturers and directed that second sources qualify by 2029. Later appropriations language included $500 million for solid-rocket-motor industrial-base modernization and expansion, with $150 million for qualification and testing of second sources. If factory capacity alone created an alternative source, separate qualification and testing funds of that scale would be unnecessary. [S28-S29]

This does not mean second sourcing will fail. It means that the relevant milestone is not “plant opened.” It is “this source, process, facility, and configuration passed the required qualification and is delivering accepted motors at rate.” The public record remains incomplete on that milestone for several entrants.

## The next bottleneck can sit upstream of the motor plant

## Ammonium perchlorate: more throughput at one source

Ammonium perchlorate is the oxidizer used in many composite solid propellants. American Pacific Corporation announced up to $100 million for a new production line at Cedar City, Utah, expected to raise capacity by more than 50 percent, with completion anticipated in 2026. Parent-company SEC disclosure corroborates the project. [S24-S25]

The expansion is material. It should improve throughput, modernization, and resilience at the facility. It does not create an independent second supplier. Throughput and source diversity are different variables. A larger single source can support more motor output while remaining a common-mode exposure to an outage, quality problem, accident, natural hazard, or input disruption.

## HTPB: a documented binder vulnerability

The Senate Armed Services Committee stated in its FY2026 report that domestic hydroxyl-terminated polybutadiene supply was fragile and relied on one U.S. production facility. HTPB is a common polymeric binder in composite propellant. The committee requested assessment and mitigation rather than treating final-motor expansion as sufficient. [S26]

The public report is not a complete market census, and the underlying briefings may contain nonpublic detail. It is still load-bearing evidence that policymakers see binder supply as a distinct constraint. A motor producer with available casting space cannot maintain rate without qualified material of the required formulation and consistency.

## Third-tier components: the source can be qualified and still constrained

The Senate Appropriations Committee reported that third-tier supply limitations continued to constrain Mk72 and Mk104 production. That observation cuts against a simple two-prime narrative. A second motor source can exist while both sources depend on the same constrained nozzle material, energetic ingredient, case component, insulation, igniter input, or test resource. [S27]

The industrial-base model therefore has at least three layers:

Investment in one layer can move the bottleneck to another. The buildout succeeds only when the entire path can support accepted deliveries.

- Installed final-motor and energetic-processing capability.
- Program-specific qualification and test capacity.
- Qualified upstream materials and third-tier components.

## What is likely to be different by 2029

A precise national output forecast would require demand by motor configuration, yield, staffing, uptime, input availability, qualification schedules, and customer acceptance. Those data are not public. A class-specific scenario is possible.

## Tactical and small motors: materially broader supply

This is the area with the strongest evidence of structural change. Anduril has an operating facility and a disclosed tactical target above 6,000 motors per year. Northrop says it has tripled tactical capacity. L3’s portfolio already includes high-volume small products. X-Bow’s modular approach and Ursa’s flexible line add possible alternatives. If announced qualification work converts, the market should look less like the two-producer structure described by GAO in 2017. [S05-S06, S13-S22]

The caveat is product specificity. A tactical motor for one missile is not a drop-in substitute for another. Supplier breadth should improve, but procurement offices will still need funded qualification and multi-year demand to keep lines economic.

## Medium and interceptor motors: more pathways, qualification determines usefulness

L3’s Virginia and Camden expansions, Northrop’s broader buildout, X-Bow’s Mk72/Mk104 work, Ursa’s 10-inch pathfinder, and congressional second-source funding create several credible pathways. The public record is stronger on physical capability and prototype work than on completed alternative-source qualification for named high-rate programs. [S05-S11, S18-S22, S28-S29]

By 2029, this class could have meaningful second-source competition if qualification milestones arrive on schedule. That is a conditional conclusion, not a counted outcome. The decisive evidence will be qualified-source announcements, definitized quantities, accepted lots, and sustained delivery rates.

## Large and strategic motors: incumbents remain central

Northrop says it has doubled large-motor production and tripled casting in Utah. L3’s Arkansas project is intended to increase large-motor capacity sixfold. Those are major changes. They are incumbent changes. Public evidence for broad entrant substitution at the large and strategic end is much thinner. [S05-S06]

Lockheed’s FTC position that entrants cannot yet produce large motors at scale should not be accepted as neutral fact; it is an interested argument. It is nevertheless consistent with the public readiness record. The entrants have disclosed facilities, modular equipment, prototypes, and tactical production. They have not publicly demonstrated a broad portfolio of sustained, qualified large-program deliveries comparable with the incumbents. [S30]

## Upstream: higher throughput, limited proof of redundancy

AMPAC’s AP expansion should raise input throughput. Congressional attention to HTPB and third-tier components shows that input fragility remains. Public evidence does not establish multiple qualified domestic sources at scale for every critical material. [S24-S27]

## 2029 scenario by constraint layer

The net result is neither “missile capacity solved” nor “nothing has changed.” The United States is moving from a brittle two-incumbent structure toward a layered market in which incumbent expansions deliver near-term volume and entrants contest selected products. The transition will be uneven. Tactical motors can become competitive before large stages. Propellant processing can expand before nozzles or binders. A second source can qualify years after its factory opens.

## A better scoreboard for the buildout

Investors, procurement officials, journalists, and analysts should stop using factory announcements as the final milestone. A useful public scoreboard would track each facility and program through a fixed sequence.

This sequence would make public claims harder to inflate and easier to update. It would also let the market recognize real progress. Anduril’s more than 700 test fires are stronger evidence than a groundbreaking. Ursa’s completed pathfinder is stronger than a capital announcement. Northrop’s completed Utah expansion is stronger than a 2029 target. L3’s THAAD framework is a better demand signal than an unsupported aspiration, but weaker than a definitized quantity and delivery schedule.

- Capital status: announced, authorized, obligated, spent.
- Construction status: ground broken, equipment installed, commissioned.
- Process evidence: first mix, first cast, static fire, repeatability, yield.
- Qualification status: configuration, customer, facility, and test campaign.
- Production instrument: prototype award, DPA agreement, framework, definitized order, lot contract, multi-year procurement.
- Output: target rate, demonstrated rate, accepted deliveries, schedule performance.
- Input coverage: AP, binder, cases, nozzles, igniters, liners, insulation, test capacity.
- Independence: whether the “second source” relies on the same constrained sub-tier source.
- Overlap: whether a facility project is already included in a company-wide investment or output target.
- Motor class and mix: tactical, medium, large, strategic, and the product distribution behind any count.

## What the audit does not establish

This research does not calculate whether future supply will meet wartime or peacetime demand. Public sources do not provide a complete demand schedule by motor configuration, current inventories, wartime expenditure assumptions, classified programs, yield, downtime, test consumption, or accepted deliveries by facility. A capacity project can be essential even when the disclosed number cannot be converted to missiles.

The readiness scores are derived judgments from public evidence. They are not engineering certifications, acquisition ratings, or claims about private capability. The taxonomy rewards sustained program-qualified production because that is what relieves a procurement bottleneck. It may understate work that companies or customers have not disclosed.

Company releases are primary evidence of what a company said and did, not independent verification of achieved rate. The audit qualifies forward-looking language, retains “more than,” “nearly,” and “up to,” and avoids converting undisclosed time bases. Government and congressional sources can also be incomplete, delayed, or based on nonpublic briefings.

The dataset does not sum capital commitments. It flags likely overlap but cannot reconstruct project cash flows from public releases. It also excludes some facilities from normalized analysis when public status, mix, or overlap cannot be resolved.

## The durable conclusion

The United States has moved beyond discussion and into a real solid-rocket-motor construction cycle. Incumbents are installing the largest immediately usable increments. New entrants have crossed different milestones: operating tactical production, commissioned modular energetics, completed pathfinders, and clean-sheet construction. Upstream oxidizer capacity is expanding. Congress is paying separately for qualification and testing.

The public capacity figures remain incompatible. Counts mix tiny and large motors. Pounds require a product mix. Multipliers omit denominators. Square footage omits throughput. Capital omits overlap. Test fires omit deliveries. A plant omits program qualification. A second motor supplier can share the same constrained sub-tier input.

By 2029, tactical and selected medium-class markets should have more supplier options than they did in 2017. Large and strategic markets will remain more dependent on the incumbents unless entrants produce public evidence of qualified, sustained deliveries. The most accurate measure of progress will not be the sum of announced “capacity.” It will be the number of named programs with multiple qualified sources, accepted lots at rate, and independent upstream supply paths.

## Methodology

The accompanying dataset contains 31 public facility-capability records and 33 sources. Each record preserves the source’s native unit, motor class, status, readiness level, capital description, likely overlap group, confidence, and limitation. No national output total is calculated. Four derived arithmetic checks are reported: Northrop’s approximate 66.7 percent propellant-capability increase; the approximate 20,000-motor 2028 comparison implied by its 2029 statement; and the two X-Bow pounds-per-equivalent ranges. All calculations are reproducible in the accompanying script and JSON output.

## Readiness taxonomy

The scale is applied by product class where the evidence permits. It is not a replacement for customer qualification data.

- 0 — Announced: concept or intention without an identified physical commitment.
- 1 — Facility: site, construction, acquisition, or installed physical capability.
- 2 — Prototype / qualification: test articles, pathfinder, or program qualification work.
- 3 — Production evidence: operating line, repeated test/production evidence, or disclosed production contract, without broad sustained program-qualified history.
- 4 — Sustained program-qualified incumbent: established qualified portfolio and deliveries.

## Measure qualified alternatives, not announced capacity

Tactical and selected medium-class supply should broaden by 2029, but announced factories do not establish qualified alternatives. Large and strategic markets remain more dependent on incumbents. Count named programs with multiple qualified sources, accepted deliveries at rate, and independent upstream supply paths.

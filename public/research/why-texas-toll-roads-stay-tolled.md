# Why Texas Toll Roads Stay Tolled
*Where the money goes after construction—and why project cost, road debt, system debt, legal tolling authority, and concession rights are five different questions.*

**Published:** September 2, 2026  
**Last verified:** September 2, 2026  
**Canonical path:** `/research/financial-systems/why-texas-toll-roads-stay-tolled`

Build the road, collect tolls, repay the road, remove the toll. That is the model many drivers carry in their heads. It describes a single project, a fixed construction bill, one loan, and a clean ending. Most large Texas toll systems do not work that way.

Consider the SH 288 managed lanes south of Houston. The original private-finance package put the project at about $1.064 billion. On October 8, 2024, TxDOT terminated the private concession and agreed to a payment of $1,731,730,721, less contractually permitted adjustments. Control transitioned to the state. The tolls did not disappear.

Texas was not buying pavement from a private road owner. The state already held public title to the corridor. It was ending a contract that carried financing, operating, maintenance, and toll-revenue rights. The payment changed the contractual and financing structure. TxDOT then continued tolling under a public structure. That transaction captures the central point: project cost, facility debt, system debt, government tolling authority, and private concession rights are separate balances and separate legal claims.

## The quick answer

Texas toll roads stay tolled for different reasons, and the correct reason depends on the exact facility. A public authority may pledge revenue across an entire network, refinance old debt, add projects, maintain required reserves, or use legally available cash for later transportation work. A private concessionaire may hold toll-revenue rights until a stated end date. A road can also remain tolled because the responsible public body still has legal authority to charge and chooses to retain the revenue stream.

“Paid off” is therefore not one financial condition. It can refer to recovery of the original construction cost, retirement of facility debt, retirement of system debt, the end of public tolling authority, or the expiration of a private contract. One of those conditions can be satisfied while the other four remain active.

- Pooled systems can use revenue from mature roads to support debt, reserves, capital work, or newer facilities.
- Debt-service coverage and reserve covenants require more revenue than bare principal and interest.
- Depreciation is not cash, while principal repayment is a cash use that does not appear as an income-statement expense.
- A public transfer to another transportation program is not economically identical to a private dividend.
- A concession normally ends on its contractual date or through a buyout—not when cumulative collections equal original construction cost.
- Texas law contains facility-specific and entity-specific rules; no universal statewide construction-cost sunset exists.

| Test | What it measures | Why tolling may continue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial project cost | Historical design, right-of-way, construction, financing, and defined later capital | Cost recovery is usually an economic calculation, not a legal sunset |
| Road-level debt | Debt specifically allocated to or secured by one road | The facility may sit inside a pooled trust even if no standalone bond remains |
| System debt and pledge | All debt, reserves, and required funds supported by pooled revenue | A mature road can support newer facilities, reconstruction, and coverage |
| Legal tolling authority | Statutory, board, county, or commission power to impose and change tolls | Debt payoff and legal authority are separate; removal may require an affirmative public action |
| Concession rights | Private operating and revenue rights through a contract term | The right can survive refinancing or bankruptcy and end only at expiry, termination, or buyout |

## “Paid off” is not one financial condition

Recovering the original construction cost is an economic-history question. It asks whether cumulative net cash collected from drivers has exceeded the defined design, right-of-way, construction, financing, and later capital cost. The answer changes with the cost definition and with whether revenue means billings, collections, or collections after operating expense.

Authorities generally do not maintain a legally controlling meter that applies each future toll receipt against one historical construction invoice. Even a careful cost-recovery calculation would not, by itself, cancel a toll.

Road-level debt is a separate financing question. A road can have no identifiable standalone bond remaining while its revenue remains pledged to a broader system. Refunding can replace old bonds with new bonds without rebuilding the road. A driver cannot infer present debt from the opening date.

System debt matters for pooled authorities. NTTA’s principal facilities share a Trust and revenue pledge. HCTRA operates a county toll system rather than independent road cash boxes. Revenue from a mature facility can support additions, connectors, reconstruction, reserves, and systemwide coverage even when the road’s initial financing is no longer outstanding.

Legal authority is another layer. Texas counties, regional tollway authorities, regional mobility authorities, and TxDOT operate under different chapters of the Transportation Code. Their boards and public governing bodies retain defined toll-setting and revenue-use powers, subject to bonds and contracts.

A private concession is a contract with a term. The project company can finance and operate the lanes, take traffic and collection risk, service project debt, and receive residual cash allowed by the agreement. The contract normally does not end when cumulative revenue equals the original build cost.

## NTTA: the road belongs to a pooled financial system

NTTA’s FY2025 audited report recorded $1.253 billion of net toll revenue, $1.105 billion of net revenue available for debt service, $675.7 million of audited debt service, and 1.64× coverage. It also reported $8.519 billion of long-term debt, with the maturity schedule extending to 2052.

Those figures describe a Trust-backed system, not one road. The Trust Agreement defines the order in which pledged revenue supports operations, debt payments, reserve requirements, subordinate obligations, and permitted capital uses. Coverage tests require revenue above bare debt service and constrain additional borrowing.

The December 2025 monthly report shows $1.3575 billion of gross Trust revenue, $252.7 million of net operating expense, and $666.9 million of preliminary cash debt service. The arithmetic difference is $437.9 million, or $32.26 per $100 of gross Trust revenue.

That remainder is an algebraic comparison output. The source schedule does not trace it to one named capital or reserve fund. It is not shareholder profit, unrestricted cash, or proof that the amount could have been returned to drivers. Any later use remains governed by the Trust and NTTA’s capital and reserve structure.

NTTA also received about $630.5 million of refunding-bond proceeds during FY2025. Refunding can change interest cost, maturity, and creditor terms. It does not mean a specific road was built twice, and it does not create an automatic toll-removal date.

## Harris County: toll-system cash can become county mobility funding

HCTRA’s FY2025 statements make the accounting-to-cash distinction visible. The accounting view begins with $1.0276 billion of toll revenue and $542.7 million of operating expense. That expense includes $115.5 million of depreciation and amortization. Depreciation allocates past capital cost; no one received a $115.5 million depreciation payment in FY2025.

The cash-flow statement shows $995.7 million collected from customers, $404.8 million paid to employees and suppliers, $242.0 million of bond principal and interest, $315.3 million of capital-asset purchases, and a $398.6 million transfer to Harris County’s thoroughfare and mobility program.

The four selected uses total $1.3607 billion, or $132.42 per $100 of reported toll revenue. That is not evidence that the authority spent the same toll dollar more than once. Beginning cash and investments, investment income, borrowing, and other sources also funded the year.

The mobility transfer is a public transfer recorded in audited county statements, not a dividend to private owners. The ACFR proves the amount and recipient. It should not be used alone to claim that the same amount was legally available for an immediate rate cut; bond covenants, reserves, capital needs, and Commissioners Court actions still matter.

At September 30, 2025, HCTRA reported $2.759 billion of bond principal, with maturities through 2054. Its revenue-bond covenant uses a 1.25× minimum under the governing calculation. The full cash-flow statement and bond documents are therefore the right evidence, not a single toll-revenue line.

## TxDOT’s debt-financed systems: excess revenue enters the reserve structure

TxDOT’s FY2025 annual toll report states that excess revenue for its debt-financed systems was deposited into project reserve accounts required by bond documents. Under that schedule, TxDOT reported no surplus from the Central Texas Turnpike System or Grand Parkway available for an unrelated transfer.

For CTTS, FY2025 operating revenue was $337.64 million. Operating expense was $101.87 million, repair and maintenance expense was $65.14 million, debt service was $160.34 million, and the residual required reserve was $10.29 million. Bonds extend to 2042.

For Grand Parkway, operating revenue was $393.32 million. Operating expense was $59.11 million, maintenance was $18.97 million, debt service was $175.38 million, and the residual required reserve was $139.86 million. Bonds extend to 2053.

A required reserve can look like money left over while functioning as pledged credit support. Bondholders lend against future tolls only if the borrower maintains specified funds, rates, and coverage. The systems also continue to absorb capital after opening through rehabilitation, widening, interchange, safety, and technology projects.

## Private concessions: project cash can reach shareholders

North Tarrant Express, LBJ Express, and NTE 35W are publicly owned corridors carrying long-term private concession rights. Their project companies operate dynamically priced managed lanes, service project debt, satisfy maintenance and distribution-lockup tests, and can distribute residual cash to private investors.

Ferrovial’s 2026 Factbook reported, at the 100% project-company level for the three Texas concessions, $935 million of FY2025 revenue, $776 million of adjusted EBITDA, and $553 million of distributions. The same filing reported $318 million as Ferrovial’s ownership-adjusted share of distributions from the Texas managed-lane companies.

Those figures use different denominators. The $553 million covers 100% of the project companies; $318 million is Ferrovial’s share. Neither is the same as payments to TxDOT.

The filing separately reported $46.7 million of FY2025 payments to TxDOT: $26.4 million of NTE 35W revenue sharing, $8.1 million of NTE revenue sharing, a $6.6 million NTE 35W refinancing-gain payment, and $5.6 million of LBJ Wishbone revenue sharing.

This is economically different from HCTRA transferring money to a county mobility program. One is a public allocation to transportation purposes. The other is a private return on invested capital after contractual claims. Both may be lawful. They should not share one label such as “surplus” or “profit.”

## SH 130: bankruptcy changed the capital, not the toll right

Texas owns SH 130 Segments 5 and 6. SH 130 Concession Company holds the long-term operating and toll-revenue rights under the Facility Concession Agreement. The original finance package totaled about $1.328 billion, including senior bank debt, a federal TIFIA loan, and private equity. The concession term extends to 2062.

The original concession company filed for Chapter 11 protection in 2016 and emerged from restructuring in 2017. The debt and ownership economics changed. The road remained open and tolled.

A bankruptcy can write down or reorganize debt without terminating the concession. A new capital structure can replace the old one while the contractual right to collect tolls survives.

Current public records identify the responsible concession entity and contract. They do not provide a clean current ultimate-equity chain comparable to Ferrovial’s public filing. This article leaves that ownership layer unresolved rather than carrying forward pre-bankruptcy sponsor names.

## SH 288: the private concession ended and tolling did not

The 2016 comprehensive development agreement gave Blueridge Transportation Group a long-term package of financing, construction, operation, maintenance, and toll-revenue rights. TxDOT retained public title to the corridor.

In 2024, the Texas Transportation Commission used the agreement’s termination-for-convenience mechanism. The stated payment was $1,731,730,721, less permitted adjustments. Control transitioned to TxDOT on October 8, 2024. Public financing entities then supported the project’s post-termination revenue and financing structure.

The state continued tolling and later reduced rates. The concession ended; the public tolling authority did not.

SH 288 shows that the relevant rights can change at different times. Texas could terminate a private contract, assume control of future revenue, and retain a tolled managed-lane model. The transaction was not proof that drivers still owed the original construction cost.

## Can a Texas toll road actually become free?

Yes. The word “never” does not survive the statute book.

Transportation Code §284.008 creates a default path for certain county toll projects. Subject to the statutory exception, a project becomes part of the state highway system and is maintained without tolls after the project-revenue bonds and interest are paid, or sufficient money is placed in trust to pay them through maturity.

Subsection (d) allows a county to request a Texas Transportation Commission order preventing that automatic transition. A February 25, 2016 Commission agenda records a Harris County request under the provision. The agenda proves the request existed; it does not establish the final order’s scope or identify every affected facility. The article therefore does not apply the exception to a named road without the underlying order.

Other entity types operate under different statutes. Section 366.407 governs surplus revenue for regional tollway authorities. Section 370.174 governs surplus revenue for regional mobility authorities. Section 228.006 governs the use of surplus revenue from TxDOT toll projects. The precise payment order and permitted use also depend on bonds, trust agreements, and project contracts.

- Relevant debt and interest must be paid or legally defeased.
- No pooled-system pledge may continue to claim the revenue.
- Reserve, operations, and lifecycle-capital obligations must be addressed.
- No concessionaire may retain a contractual toll right.
- The responsible public body must have authority to remove the toll and choose to act.
- Another funding source must accept future operations and reconstruction.

| Structure | Primary law | Decision-maker | What debt payoff does not do automatically |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| County toll project | Transportation Code Chapter 284 and §284.008 | Commissioners court and, for statutory transition, Texas Transportation Commission | Does not resolve a subsection (d) order, pooled obligations, reserves, or future maintenance funding |
| Regional tollway authority | Chapter 366 and §366.407 | Authority board under bond and trust covenants | Does not extinguish continuing toll power or permitted system uses |
| Regional mobility authority | Chapter 370 and §370.174 | RMA board under bond documents | Does not decide the order among toll reduction, reserves, and other transportation projects |
| TxDOT toll project | Chapter 228 and §228.006 | Texas Transportation Commission and related financing entities | Does not remove public tolling authority or project reserve requirements |
| Traffic-risk concession | Executed concession agreement | Public owner plus contractual rights and remedies | Does not end the private term unless the contract provides for expiry, termination, or buyout |

## Who can change or remove the toll?

The decision-maker depends on the structure. NTTA rates run through the NTTA board and Trust covenants. HCTRA policy runs through Harris County government and its bond documents. CTRMA’s board acts under Chapter 370 and its financing agreements. CTTS, Grand Parkway, and SH 288 sit under the Texas Transportation Commission and related public financing structures. A private concessionaire may price within the formulas, caps, performance standards, and public controls in its contract.

A campaign promise, press release, or historical statement about toll removal is not a covenant. The enforceable evidence is the statute, bond resolution, trust agreement, rate covenant, concession agreement, and board or commission action.

The better test for any facility asks which entity can charge today, what revenue pledge applies, which debt and reserves still claim the cash, whether residual cash can be transferred, whether a concession term remains, which public body could act, and what funding source would replace toll revenue after removal.

- What entity has the legal right to impose the toll today?
- Is revenue pledged road-by-road, project-by-project, or systemwide?
- What debt, interest, reserve, and coverage requirements remain?
- Can residual cash be transferred, and for what legally permitted purpose?
- Is there a private concession term, termination formula, or reversion date?
- Which board, court, or commission could reduce or remove the toll?
- What source would pay future operations and reconstruction after toll removal?

## Method, limitations, and correction route

The analysis uses the latest audited fiscal year available by September 2, 2026, supplemented by statutory toll reports, bond and trust disclosures, monthly operating reports, commission actions, and concession documents. Figures are not combined across systems unless the fiscal year, denominator, and accounting basis are visible.

The accounting view keeps operating revenue, operating expense, depreciation, interest expense, and change in net position separate. The cash and claims view keeps customer receipts, cash operating payments, principal, cash interest, capital purchases, reserve movements, transfers, borrowing, and ending cash separate.

Depreciation is not cash. Principal is not an income-statement expense. Net position is not a bank balance. Current capital purchases may be funded by current toll receipts, old reserves, grants, investment proceeds, or new debt.

## Frequently asked questions

Where does Texas toll money go? Depending on the system, it supports collection and customer service, road operations, routine and major maintenance, principal and interest, required reserves, new capital work, public transportation transfers, government revenue sharing, or private distributions after senior claims.

Do Texas toll roads make a profit? Public authorities can generate revenue above current operating and debt-service claims, but that amount is not shareholder profit. It may remain subject to bond covenants, reserves, capital programs, and statutory uses. Private concession companies can distribute cash to shareholders after contractual and financing restrictions.

Why do tolls keep increasing? The answer is facility-specific. Rate schedules can be linked to inflation, statutory or board policies, dynamic congestion formulas, debt-service and coverage covenants, concession terms, and projected capital needs.

Can toll revenue fund other roads? In pooled public systems, revenue can support the system rather than one facility. Some statutes and public actions also permit specified transportation transfers after required obligations are satisfied.

What happens when a concession ends? The private operating and revenue rights expire or are terminated under the contract. Public ownership may have existed throughout. The public body then decides the post-concession operating and toll structure subject to law and financing.

Was SH 288 bought back? Texas terminated the private concession and paid for the remaining contractual rights and financing consequences. The state already owned the highway corridor. Tolling continued after the private right ended.

## Conclusion

**The road is not the balance sheet**

A defensible answer has to name the exact road or system, its current debt and pledge, the governing statute or contract, the authority that can change the rate, and the funding source that would replace toll revenue. “The road was built years ago” answers none of those questions.

# Adobe: Paid Workflows at a Price I Like

*Written October 13, 2026. Research and entry-price cutoff: October 12, 2026. ADBE entry close: $238.14. Forecast evaluation: October 12, 2028.*

I would buy Adobe at $238.14. My central forecast is **$365 by October 12, 2028**, roughly 53% above the entry price. I believe the business can keep getting paid for the work surrounding an image, video or document as the cost of generating the first version falls.

That belief has to earn its place in the numbers. My base case assumes 8% annual revenue growth, a cash margin below the current trailing figure, and repurchases funded after an economic charge for employee equity and a separate capital budget. It does not require Adobe to win every contest between AI models. It requires customers to keep paying for useful creative and document workflows.

The largest risk is straightforward: those workflows could become cheaper and easier to replace, reducing both the number of paid users and the price Adobe can charge. I am buying at a valuation that gives that risk some room. I am not assuming it disappears.

## Where I think Adobe can keep earning its money

An image that looks good in a chat window is an intermediate product. A commercial campaign may need editable source files, consistent characters, several aspect ratios, approved fonts, localized copy and dozens of versions that can be revised again. A video still needs a sequence someone can change. A document still has to be understood, shared and acted on.

My investment hypothesis is that cheaper generation increases the volume of this work enough to support spending on the software that finishes it. More output creates more production and review tasks. Adobe can earn money by making that process faster, even when the underlying model comes from another company.

This is a specific hypothesis about customer behavior. A designer becoming twice as productive could mean a larger campaign, or it could mean an employer buys half as many seats. The first outcome helps Adobe; the second threatens its revenue. I want paid growth and cash conversion to decide between them.

Adobe's own architecture permits both its Firefly models and partner models inside parts of its product suite. In June it announced AI assistants in public beta across Photoshop, Premiere, Illustrator, InDesign and Frame.io, with workflows such as reorganizing layers, assembling an initial edit and producing versions of a file. The unified Firefly studio remained a private-beta preview. Those product states matter: a launch establishes availability, not retention or profitable adoption. [Annual product description](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/796343/000079634326000003/adbe-20251128.htm), [June creative-agent announcement](https://news.adobe.com/news/2026/06/adobe-unveils-major-expansion)

I like the direction because it puts new capability inside work people already perform. The economic test is whether that capability protects paid demand, creates valuable additional usage or raises the cost of switching. An impressive demonstration alone cannot answer it.

## The paid business is still growing

The current evidence supports continued demand. It does not yet prove the full AI thesis.

Adobe's fiscal third quarter ended August 28. Revenue reached $6.760 billion, versus $5.988 billion a year earlier. Customer-group subscription revenue was $4.651 billion for Creative & Marketing Professionals and $1.905 billion for Business Professionals & Consumers, with reported growth of 13% and 16%. The latter group gives the thesis a second source of demand beyond professional creative applications. ## I will not let an AI headline carry the valuation

Adobe disclosed AI-first ARR above $650 million and growth above 150%. It also reported more than a billion monthly active users across creativity and productivity products. These are encouraging measures of reach and activity. They are different from the paid economics I am underwriting. [Management's prepared remarks](https://www.adobe.com/cc-shared/assets/investor-relations/pdfs/01906202/c6yetrerew.pdf)

The $650 million is a floor. Dividing it by total ARR produces a 2.36% lower-bound comparison, not an exact share of revenue or an upper bound on the AI business. Nor does that disclosure isolate how much AI revenue replaces an existing subscription, what serving it costs, or how much profit it creates. The billion users include free activity; they are not a billion paying Creative Cloud customers.

I give the company credit for building distribution. I give the valuation credit only when that distribution becomes paid demand and cash. This keeps the long useful even if the most exciting adoption statistic slows. It also prevents a successful free product from disguising weakness in the subscription business.

The base forecast consequently uses consolidated revenue growth below the latest reported pace. It does not extrapolate 150% growth, add an invented AI revenue stream, or assume that a new interface expands margins automatically.

I also expect the subscription book to give warning before every problem reaches earnings. Revenue recognized across an existing contract can remain healthy while the next renewal becomes harder. ARR and contracted obligations offer another view, although price, currency and acquisitions affect them too. If quarterly revenue keeps meeting guidance while these forward measures slow materially, I would examine the paid-demand thesis before celebrating EPS helped by repurchases. A useful forecast should make me change my interpretation early enough for that change to matter.

## The cash bridge makes the entry attractive

The latest trailing year produced $10.806 billion of operating cash. Cash capital expenditure of $214 million leaves $10.592 billion of free cash flow. I then charge the $2.071 billion of stock compensation added back in operating cash flow, producing an **$8.521 billion cash-after-compensation proxy**.

Stock compensation is not an immediate cash payment. This deduction treats employee equity as an economic cost before I ask how much cash can support a smaller share count. It is a conservative sensitivity, not Adobe's reported metric or an exact estimate of the stock purchases required to replace dilution.

Using the 389.2 million shares disclosed for September 18, the entry price implies approximately $92.684 billion of equity value. The proxy represents a 9.19% yield, or 10.88 times annual cash. The share denominator is dated; the exact October 12 count is not public. I also cross-check earnings: the entry is about 13.1 times the midpoint of FY2026 GAAP EPS guidance, $18.145. [Share count and cash statements](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/796343/000079634326000156/adbe-20260828.htm), [EPS guidance](https://www.adobe.com/cc-shared/assets/investor-relations/pdfs/01906202/au56y4ter.pdf)

Those valuations appeal to me because they attach a manageable price to a large existing earnings base. The favorable outcome does not have to come from a distant product becoming dominant. It can come from established demand surviving better than the entry valuation allows.

## Fewer shares need a cash budget

Repurchases strengthen this case only when they create affordable net retirement. In the first nine months, balance-sheet shares fell from 413 million to 391 million, a 5.33% reduction, while Adobe spent $6.821 billion on repurchases. That is evidence of a shrinking denominator. It is not a separate return to add to a target price already calculated with fewer shares.

My base case asks for 4% net retirement in each model year. At assumed average repurchase prices of $285 and $330, that requires $4.437 billion and $4.932 billion of net-purchase funding. Rising prices make later retirement more expensive; the model recognizes that.

A company can also destroy value through acquisitions or poorly priced repurchases. This cash allocation leaves room to absorb mistakes without assuming debt finances a permanent reduction in shares.

## How I get to $365

My base case grows the $25.970 billion revenue anchor by 8% annually, reaching a $30.291 billion annual run rate near the evaluation date. Cash after compensation equals 31% of sales, below the observed trailing margin of roughly 32.8%. That produces $9.390 billion of annual cash rather than an assumption of expanding cash profitability.

With 358.687 million ending shares, the result is $26.18 per share. Fourteen times that cash produces **$366.52 in the model**, which I register as a rounded $365 central forecast. A separate 27% net-margin cross-check produces about $22.80 of earnings per share; the exact model price is roughly 16.1 times those earnings. The cross-check does not get added to the cash valuation.

The revaluation matters. Keeping the entry cash multiple would produce about $284.76, a 19.6% price gain. My central forecast requires buyers to pay 14 times cash rather than today's 10.88. I believe durable paid growth could justify that change. It is a judgment about future valuation, and it can be wrong even if the business performs reasonably well.

The reverse calculation shows the condition clearly. With the base share count and a 14-times multiple, Adobe needs about $7.383 billion of annual cash to support a 10% annualized price return from the entry. That is 13.4% below the observed proxy. This room exists because the calculation assumes both net retirement and a higher valuation multiple; it is not protection against every form of deterioration.

## The alternatives are meaningfully different

| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Bear | −4% | 25% | 0% | 9× | $138.36 |
| Base | +8% | 31% | 4% | 14× | $366.52 |
| Bull | +12% | 33% | 6% | 17× | $531.43 |

The bear case is severe enough to represent structural erosion: revenue falls in both years, the cash margin contracts, and net retirement stops. Its roughly $140 rounded outcome would be a substantial loss. Current cash generation cannot make that combination harmless.

The bull case needs paid expansion, sustained cash conversion and faster retirement despite higher purchase prices. Its roughly $530 outcome is possible within the model, but I do not give it a probability or let it substitute for the central case.

Holding base operating assumptions constant, 11 times cash gives approximately $288, while 17 times gives $445. This spread tells me that recognition by future buyers matters almost as much as modestly changing the growth forecast. It also tells me why excellent software can still produce a disappointing stock return when bought at the wrong price.

## What I will judge before the horizon

I want the latest available FY2028 results to show an annual revenue run rate above $30 billion, cash after the same compensation charge above $9 billion, and comparable ARR growth of at least 7%. I also expect net retirement near the base case's 7.84% over two model years, achieved without relying on expanding debt. These are my prospective tests.

The clearest disconfirmation would be two successive reports with ARR growth below 5%, GAAP operating margin below 30%, or trailing cash after compensation below $7.5 billion. A growing free audience alongside those outcomes would strengthen the contrary case. So would repeated acquisitions that consume the cash budget without improving paid demand.

Quarterly results are the principal catalyst: the company has to show that useful paid workflows survive changing technology. The scheduled December 1 CEO transition to Anil Chakravarthy offers another point at which strategy and capital allocation may become clearer, but leadership change alone earns no additional value in my model. [Transition announcement](https://news.adobe.com/news/2026/09/adobe-announces-anil-chakravarthy-to-become-president-and-ceo)

I believe in Adobe's ability to adapt, and I like the price attached to that belief. The prediction remains concrete: $365 by October 12, 2028. I will compare the stock's price return with SPY over the same dates and retain this original forecast when the thesis changes. The business must keep getting paid; the investment must reward the price I am willing to pay today.

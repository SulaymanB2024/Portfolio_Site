# Sell the Review Before You Scale the App

If I were launching an inventory app for Shopify merchants, I would start with a paid, assisted review. I would sit with the person responsible for replenishment, help them complete a real purchasing decision, and charge for a repeatable workflow. The first thing I would try to learn is whether that decision is valuable enough to support the business.

That choice changes what I build. An app sold for $99 has to make setup and routine support cheap. A $399 assisted offer can afford more work, but it has to produce more value. An agency can introduce customers, but its commission only makes sense if those introductions solve a constraint I actually have. A growth channel carries an implied product, price, and operating model with it.

I built a six-period model to make those commitments visible. The assisted offer looks strongest in the starting scenario. Then I hold price constant, and the ranking reverses. That reversal is the most useful result: the spreadsheet gives me a hypothesis to test, and also shows where I have quietly assumed the answer.

## Start with the decision the merchant already makes

The hypothetical customer here is a merchant with several suppliers, physical inventory, and one person accountable for a weekly replenishment review. The proposed product combines inventory data with supplier lead times, surfaces a short list of exceptions, and records an owner and decision date. The merchant retains purchasing authority. I am assuming an approved, working public app and $40,000 left to fund the next six 30-day periods; the earlier development bill is outside this decision.

Shopify already provides sell-through, inventory coverage, and ABC analysis. An additional chart would have to compete with those native tools. I would begin by watching the operator use what they already have: which supplier dates need reconciliation, which exceptions lead to action, and where the review ends without a decision. [Shopify product analytics](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/analytics), [inventory reports](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/reports-and-analytics/shopify-reports/report-types/default-reports/inventory-reports).

The distinction matters because preparation time and decision quality are different sources of value. A report can save ten minutes and still leave the difficult judgment untouched. A workflow can make the review more orderly without changing what gets purchased. I would want a customer to show me the previous process, the completed decision, and the work the new product actually replaces.

For the first offer, I would keep the promise narrow: one store, one weekly review, merchant-supplied lead times, and a reusable exception process. I would record requests for custom purchasing services or an ERP replacement, but exclude that work from the standard price. Otherwise, a successful sale could hide a different business inside every account.

## The higher price has to earn its place

In the model, self-service costs $99 per 30 days and assisted direct costs $399. The agency offer also costs $399, with 25% of gross app billings paid to the agency. These are proposed prices, not observed willingness to pay. I do not have evidence that the same merchant would accept all three offers.

Suppose four reviews in a period each save an hour of an operator's time, valued at an assumed $60 an hour. That is $240 of released capacity. It does not cover a $399 subscription on its own. If the offer is worth more, I need to identify the additional benefit: perhaps an avoided rush purchase, fewer repeated exceptions, or a decision that previously went unmade. I would verify that benefit against the merchant's own records. I would not credit the app with every stockout that happens to disappear during a pilot.

Even the $240 is a value calculation, not necessarily cash savings. A salaried operator may use those hours elsewhere without reducing payroll. That can still be useful, but the buyer has to prefer that use of time enough to pay. The product's value depends on the work the merchant can now complete, not on a theoretical wage multiplied by every minute the app touches.

I would therefore sell the assisted offer with a defined exit: after setup, the merchant should be able to complete the review without a founder interpreting every result. Assistance is useful if it teaches us how to make the workflow repeatable. If it remains essential to every decision, $399 may be the wrong price for the service we are delivering.

## Put the work created by each sale into the plan

The team in this example has two founders. One has 60 hours per period for onboarding and support after reserving time for product work. The other has 40 commercial hours. Fixed cash outflow is $6,000 per period, comprising $5,000 in total founder draws and $1,000 in baseline operations. Each billed store adds an assumed $12 of variable cost. A $10,000 cash reserve is the stop line.

All three motions use the same approved public app and compliant billing. Direct selling does not change the app's distribution obligations. Shopify's current terms specify a 2.9% processing fee and, for eligible developers, 0% revenue share on the first $1 million of cumulative gross app revenue earned from January 1, 2025, across associated apps and accounts. The base case assumes eligibility below that threshold; the model also tests a 15% platform share. [Distribution](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/launch/distribution), [billing requirements](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/launch/shopify-app-store/app-store-requirements), [revenue share](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/launch/distribution/revenue-share).

This is why a customer target alone is a poor operating plan. A retained customer contributes revenue and reserves future work. The support assumption can become more consequential than the acquisition assumption once enough cohorts accumulate.

## What the starting scenario actually says

The model applies churn before adding each period's new stores. It bills each admitted store for a full period, pays current acquisition and variable costs immediately, and receives net app collections one period later. The 30-day billing interval is available in Shopify's subscription system; the one-period collection lag is my planning assumption, not a promised payout schedule. Actual payouts depend on merchant payment and partner payout timing. [Subscription billing](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/launch/billing/manual-pricing/subscription-billing), [partner payments](https://help.shopify.com/en/partners/getting-started/getting-paid).

## Hold price constant before calling it a channel advantage

The starting comparison gives direct sales a price four times as high as self-service. It also assumes better retention and lower ongoing support. Those differences may be earned by a better-defined customer and more careful setup. They may also be assumptions that make my preferred option win.

I reran the model with direct priced at $99, leaving its other inputs unchanged. It ends with $4,649, less than self-service's $6,927 at the same price. I also priced self-service at $399 while retaining its original demand, churn, and support inputs. It ends with $56,048, far above the original direct result. Neither scenario establishes a feasible commercial offer; both expose the importance of price to the ranking.

The test I need is therefore more specific than “does direct selling work?” I need merchants to pay for the assisted scope, renew after they understand it, and complete reviews with less founder involvement. If that happens, the offer may earn its price and support advantage. If it does not, the comparison gives me no reason to prefer direct.

I would avoid testing this by giving one offer to large merchants and another to tiny stores, then attributing the difference to the sales motion. I would record store complexity, purchasing frequency, existing tools, accountable buyer, offered scope, and price for each account. With a small pilot, I cannot estimate a precise channel effect. I can still identify whether the underlying customer and product promise are coherent.

## A good partner can change the order

The agency's base case includes two periods of recruitment before referrals begin. If a qualified agency is already ready, with modeled potential starts of four, then eight per period, ending cash rises to $28,210. That exceeds assisted direct. The conclusion changes because the partner removes the wait for demand, not because a referral is inherently a better customer.

I would also ask what the 25% commission buys. In the starting direct plan, commercial time is not full. Reducing sales hours from three to one per new store has limited immediate value if those saved hours do not produce something useful. Reliable access to suitable merchants can justify a commission; unused sales capacity cannot justify it by itself.

The apparent alternative of automating self-service also needs a cash check. Reducing onboarding to half an hour and support to a quarter-hour admits all 96 modeled starts. Ending cash falls to $4,306. Faster admission advances acquisition spending before enough retained billings arrive to repay it. I would still want cheaper delivery, but I would pace acquisition while the lower-priced offer proves that it can fund growth.

## Buy the next answer before buying more growth

For the first two periods, I would aim for the modeled three and four assisted starts. The company has to fund $13,169 of gross outflow during that interval, partly offset by collections. That is the cost of the operating test in this scenario. It gives me a concrete budget against which to judge the information gained.

Each account would have a baseline review, a defined exception workflow, and a record of time spent by both merchant and founders. I would track whether the merchant acted, whether the workflow repeated, which decisions still required interpretation, and whether the merchant renewed at the quoted price. A founder completing the review successfully is weaker evidence than the merchant completing it successfully.

The numerical sensitivities explain why I would keep those gates. At $299, holding the base customer path fixed, direct's sixth-period surplus falls to $433. At $249 it becomes an $820 shortfall. Keeping the $399 price but finding only three new starts every period also leaves period six just below break-even. The business cannot solve weak willingness to pay simply by being efficient at onboarding.

Those price tests hold quantities fixed; a real price change can alter conversion, retention, support expectations, and acquisition cost. I would update those inputs together once paid use provides evidence. I would not lower price in the model, preserve every favorable customer assumption, and call the result conservative.

## The product I would try to earn

I would start with assisted direct because it gives this hypothetical team a workable budget and a chance to see the decision closely. I would spend that proximity on learning how to remove founder involvement. The offer would need to survive native substitutes, a real price, renewal, and the merchant's ability to run the process.

If a ready agency brings suitable buyers sooner, I would change the sequence. If merchants want a cheap tool they can use unaided, I would redesign setup, support, and acquisition spending around that offer. If the work stays bespoke, I would price it as bespoke work or stop pretending it is a scalable app.

The channel choice is useful when it forces those commitments into the open. It determines whose problem I see, what I can charge, what each customer expects, and which work compounds as the customer base grows. I would choose the first channel for the business it lets me test, then make the next growth decision from what paid use has taught me.

11 / Evidence

SH 130: bankruptcy changed the capital, not the toll right

Texas owns SH 130 Segments 5 and 6. SH 130 Concession Company holds the long-term operating and toll-revenue rights under the Facility Concession Agreement.

The original financing package totaled about $1.328 billion, including senior bank debt, a federal TIFIA loan, and private equity. The concession term extends to 2062.

The original project company filed for Chapter 11 protection in 2016 and emerged from restructuring in 2017. Debt and ownership economics changed. The road remained open and tolled.

A bankruptcy can write down or reorganize debt without terminating the concession. A new capital structure can replace the old one while the toll right survives.

Current public records identify the responsible concession entity and contract. They do not provide a clean ultimate-equity chain comparable to Ferrovial's public filing. This article leaves that ownership layer unresolved rather than carrying forward pre-bankruptcy names.

12 / Evidence

SH 288: the concession ended and the toll did not

The 2016 comprehensive development agreement gave Blueridge Transportation Group a long-term package of financing, construction, operation, maintenance, and toll-revenue rights. TxDOT retained public title.

In 2024, the Texas Transportation Commission used the agreement's termination-for-convenience mechanism. The stated payment was $1,731,730,721, less permitted adjustments. Control transitioned to the public structure on October 8, 2024.

Texas later reduced rates and retained the tolled managed-lane model. The private concession ended; public tolling authority did not.

The transaction was not proof that drivers still owed the original construction cost. Texas paid to end a private contract, assumed control of future revenue, and chose to keep that revenue inside a public project structure.

13 / Evidence

A Texas toll road can become free

The word never does not survive the statute book. Transportation Code §284.008 creates a default path for certain county toll projects. Subject to the statutory exception, a project becomes part of the state highway system and is maintained without tolls after project-revenue bonds and interest are paid, or sufficient money is placed in trust to pay them through maturity.

Subsection (d) lets a county request a Texas Transportation Commission order preventing that automatic transition. A February 25, 2016 Commission agenda records a Harris County request under the provision. The agenda proves the request existed; it does not identify the final order's complete scope. This article does not assign the exception to a named road without that order.

Other structures use different provisions. Section 366.407 addresses surplus revenue for regional tollway authorities. Section 370.174 addresses regional mobility authorities. Section 228.006 addresses surplus revenue from TxDOT toll projects. Bond documents and contracts may further constrain each statute.

  • Relevant debt and interest must be paid or legally defeased.
  • No pooled-system pledge may continue to claim the revenue.
  • Reserve, operating, and lifecycle-capital obligations must be addressed.
  • No concessionaire may retain a contractual toll right.
  • The responsible public body must have authority to remove the toll and choose to exercise it.
  • Another source must accept future operations and reconstruction costs.
14 / Evidence

Who can change or remove the toll

The decision-maker follows the legal structure. NTTA rates run through the NTTA board and Trust covenants. HCTRA policy runs through Harris County government and bond documents. CTRMA's board acts under Chapter 370. CTTS, Grand Parkway, and SH 288 sit under the Texas Transportation Commission and related public financing entities.

A private concessionaire may price within the formula, caps, performance standards, and public controls in its contract. A campaign promise, press release, or historical statement about toll removal is not a covenant.

Method, evidence limits, and correction route

The analysis uses the latest audited fiscal year available by September 2, 2026, supplemented by bond disclosures, monthly reports, statutes, commission actions, and concession documents. Figures are not combined across systems without the fiscal year and accounting basis shown.

The accounting view keeps operating revenue, operating expense, depreciation, interest expense, and change in net position separate. The cash and claims view keeps customer receipts, operating payments, principal, cash interest, capital purchases, reserve movements, transfers, borrowing, and ending cash separate.

Four items remain on the fact-check register: the final Harris County §284.008(d) order and project list, the current ultimate-equity chain for SH 130 Concession Company, a complete CTRMA audit-to-monthly category bridge, and exact page-level FBGPTRA headline extraction.

None is filled with an estimate. Corrections should record the date, affected claim, old value, new value, source, and whether the conclusion changed.

16 / Evidence

The better questions to ask about any toll road

The statement that a road was built years ago cannot resolve its current financing or legal status. These seven questions can.

  • Which entity has the legal right to impose the toll today?
  • Is the revenue pledged road-by-road, project-by-project, or systemwide?
  • What debt, interest, reserve, and coverage requirements remain?
  • Can residual cash be transferred, and for what legally permitted purpose?
  • Does a private concession have a term, termination formula, or reversion date?
  • Which board, court, or commission could lower or remove the toll?
  • What source would pay future operations and reconstruction after toll removal?

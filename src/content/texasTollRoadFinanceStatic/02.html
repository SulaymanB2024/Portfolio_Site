05 / Evidence

Recovering construction cost does not retire every claim

A project-cost recovery calculation asks whether cumulative net cash collected from drivers has exceeded the original design and construction cost. The answer changes with the definition of cost: right-of-way, capitalized interest, grants, financing fees, electronic collection, later widening, and rehabilitation may or may not be included.

Authorities generally do not maintain a legally controlling meter that applies each future toll receipt against one historical construction invoice. Even a careful historical answer does not usually cancel a toll.

A road may have no identifiable standalone bond remaining while its revenue supports a pooled system. Refunding bonds can replace prior debt to alter rates, maturities, or creditor terms. That is a financing change, not a second construction project.

06 / Evidence

NTTA: the toll belongs to a pooled Trust

NTTA's FY2025 audited report recorded $1.253 billion of net toll revenue, $1.105 billion of net revenue available for debt service, $675.7 million of audited debt service, 1.64 times coverage, and $8.519 billion of long-term debt. The debt schedule extends to 2052.

Those figures describe a Trust, not one road. Pledged revenue supports system operations and required debt payments before later uses. Coverage tests require revenue above bare debt service. Additional borrowing, reserve releases, and subordinate obligations remain governed by the Trust Agreement.

The December 2025 monthly report shows $1.3575 billion of gross Trust revenue, $252.7 million of net operating expense, and $666.9 million of preliminary cash debt service. The arithmetic remainder is $437.9 million.

That $437.9 million is an algebraic remainder in this comparison. The schedule does not trace it to one named fund. It is not labeled unrestricted cash, profit, a reserve deposit, or a capital allocation. Any later use remains subject to the pooled Trust.

NTTA also received about $630.5 million of refunding-bond proceeds during FY2025. Refunding changes creditor claims, maturities, and interest cost. It does not establish that a road was built twice or create an automatic toll-removal date.

07 / Evidence

HCTRA: toll-system cash can support county mobility work

The mobility transfer is the clearest public-system answer to where money can go after construction. Harris County used a material amount of toll-system cash for a broader transportation program. The audited record identifies the amount and recipient; it does not support calling the transfer an owner withdrawal.

At September 30, 2025, HCTRA reported $2.759 billion of bond principal with final maturities reaching 2054. Its revenue-bond covenant requires at least 1.25 times coverage under the governing calculation. Some obligations also have security or support features that differ from a pure standalone revenue bond.

The policy question remains open: more cash could remain in the toll system, support reserves and capital, fund county mobility work, or potentially affect rates. The accounting records the decision made. It does not decide which policy is preferable.

08 / Evidence

TxDOT's financed systems: excess enters the reserve structure

TxDOT's FY2025 toll report states that excess revenue for its debt-financed systems was deposited into project reserve accounts required by the bond documents. Under that schedule, CTTS and Grand Parkway reported no surplus available for an unrelated transfer.

Reserve and coverage requirements are part of the financing bargain. Investors lend against toll revenue because the project promises to maintain rates, coverage, and specified funds. A reserve can look like leftover money to a driver while remaining committed credit support.

The roads also keep absorbing capital after opening. TxDOT's FY2026-FY2030 planning records list rehabilitation, widening, interchange, technology, and safety work. Opening day is not the end of a road's capital life.

09 / Evidence

CTRMA and Fort Bend are included without invented comparability

CTRMA's FY2025 audit reported $276.4 million of toll revenue, about $2.448 billion of debt principal, and 2.28 times debt-service coverage. Its June 2025 statements provide more operating and debt detail, but the categories do not bridge cleanly to the audit without assumptions. CTRMA remains outside the normalized chart.

Fort Bend County has two separate toll authorities. FBCTRA reported $68.54 million of FY2025 toll revenue and $526.91 million of bond principal. Its $43.0 million of total expense includes noncash and financing-related items, so it is not used as cash O&M.

FBGPTRA publishes a separate FY2025 report and has its own financing structure. Combining the authorities or substituting countywide figures would make the analysis less accurate, not more complete.

10 / Evidence

Private concessions can distribute cash to shareholders

North Tarrant Express, LBJ Express, and NTE 35W are publicly owned corridors with long-term private concession rights. Their project companies finance and operate dynamically priced managed lanes, service project debt, and may distribute cash after satisfying contractual and financing tests.

Ferrovial's 2026 Factbook reported, at the 100 percent project-company level for the three Texas concessions, $935 million of FY2025 revenue, $776 million of adjusted EBITDA, and $553 million of project-company distributions. The same filing reported $318 million as Ferrovial's ownership-adjusted share of distributions.

Those are different denominators. The first set covers the complete project companies; the second is Ferrovial's share. Neither is the same as payments to TxDOT.

The filing separately reported $46.7 million of FY2025 payments to TxDOT: $26.4 million of NTE 35W revenue sharing, $8.1 million from NTE, a $6.6 million NTE 35W refinancing-gain payment, and $5.6 million of LBJ Wishbone revenue sharing.

The path is not toll booth to dividend. Debt service, distribution lockups, capital needs, maintenance standards, and public-agency sharing come first. The records nevertheless establish that a traffic-risk concession can distribute cash to private investors.
